AWhy a city exists, and where

A.1Definitions of a city

There is no single definition of a city. Choose the definition that fits your question:

  • Administrative: a government's territory.
  • Physical: the built-up area.
  • Population: population density.
  • Function: the area connected by commuting, trade, and services.
  • Culture/experience: a shared sense of place.

A.1.1Functional and Tributary AreasTerm listSlides 1b

Functional and Tributary Areas means defining a city by what it does. These are the areas that "belong" to it: its commuting zone, TV market, sports fans, or medical service area.

A.2Land price and location theories

A.2.1Land price versus travel costSlides 2a

A city's pattern comes from millions of small decisions, such as where to open a firm or rent a home. Each person maximizes "utility"—money, time, and enjoyment—by making trade-offs.

  • Central locations have the highest land price and lowest travel cost.
  • Outer locations have the lowest land price and highest travel cost.

The system settles into a balance, or equilibrium. Every theory below applies this idea.

A.2.3Von Thünen modelTerm listSlides 2a

The first theory linking transport and land use. Farmers locate around a market by weighing land rent against the cost of getting goods there. Profit = selling price − production cost − transport cost, and transport cost rises with distance. Heavy, profitable goods are therefore produced close to the market; light, land-hungry goods farther away. The same trade-offs underlie modern urban economics.

A.2.4Central Place TheoryTerm listSlides 2a

Towns form a hierarchy. A larger central place supplies specialized goods to everyone nearby (few people want them, so they travel farther). Everyday goods such as groceries come from many small places. So large centers are few and far apart, and small towns are numerous and close together. Chicago complicates the picture: it grew first, and smaller cities followed.

A.2.5Sector and Multiple Nuclei modelsTerm listSlides 2a

The Hoyt sector model: similar development follows transport corridors such as rail lines and highways, not straight-line distance from the center. The Multiple Nuclei model (Harris and Ullman): a city has several centers instead of one CBD. Some land uses attract each other and some repel each other (homes and factories). Single-CBD cities grow into each other, so the model fits the post-highway city better.

A.2.6Alonso-Muth-Mills model (intracity)Term listSlides 2a

Everyone works in the CBD, and commuting cost rises with distance from it. People trade cheaper housing farther out against a longer commute, so rent and density fall with distance. The model predicts rents, densities and building heights well, and can be extended to several CBDs. In the US, lower-income households tend to consume less housing to live closer to the center (Dexter).

A.2.7Rosen-Roback model (intercity)Term listSlides 2a

The same logic across metropolitan areas, with free movement between them. Wages, rents, productivity and amenities balance out. High rent with high wages means high productivity. High rent with low wages means high amenities. Low rent with low wages means low productivity or low amenities.

A.3Clustering and transport change

A.3.1AgglomerationTerm listSlides 2a

Agglomeration explains why some cities are more productive. It involves clustering (lower travel cost), specialization (higher wages), and knowledge spillovers; it is also cultural. One innovation can push out others (Ford, Detroit).

Memory tip: three ways nearness pays: Cluster, Specialize, Spill over (knowledge).

A.4Reading: Dexter, Transportation and Land Use

A.4.1Land use and transport shape each otherDexter

Dexter calls the link a duality. Transport can drive land use, and land use drives the need for transport. Where and what is built decides how people and goods travel, and the transport network decides where building happens.

Memory tip: duality = a two-way street: transport shapes land, land shapes transport.

A.4.2Cheaper transport pulls people and firms outwardDexter

The standard urban model's main point: lower transport cost, in time or money, entices people away from the center. Faster expressways and rail lines have reduced population density in the CBD. Land close to transport improvements is in greater demand, and anything that speeds access to the CBD can mean more sprawl.

A.4.3Firms: Weber, central places and tradeTerm listDexter

Weber: firms choose the location with the lowest overall cost, counting transport, labor and agglomeration. Co-locating with similar industry shares resources and knowledge. Trade theory: transport is typically the biggest location cost. Falling transport costs plus communications technology let firms become efficient and build complex global supply chains. Firms move closer to workers (and decentralize) when moving goods costs less than commuting, and closer to customers when sales are time-sensitive.

A.4.4From the pedestrian era to the freeway eraTerm listDexter

In the pedestrian and streetcar era, CBDs concentrated work, and people who could afford it moved outward; streetcars sent all but the poorest to the outskirts. In the freeway era, cars made the middle class far more mobile and suburbs grew faster than central cities. Transit ridership fell, so governments had to take over many lines. Polycentric cities grew out of sprawl as suburbs became self-contained sectors with their own downtowns, and freeway intersections became employment centers. Freeways also created logistics hubs: warehouses on cheap peripheral land.

A.4.5Accessibility and congestionDexter

Accessibility is the number of jobs a person can reach in a set time, typically 30 minutes or 1 hour. Faster travel raises it; congestion lowers it. More accessible land is more desirable and more costly. Dexter notes a link between rising congestion and weaker economic output in Southern California.

A.4.6Spatial mismatch and transitDexter

Spatial mismatch is directly related to a lack of accessibility. One solution is mixed-use development with mass transit, which can cut vehicle miles traveled and free up parking space. US transit use is low and usually subsidized.

A.4.7Transport investment and land valueDexter

Transport investment raises property values and development, and even an announcement can. But "build it and they will come": a wider freeway is congested again once volumes rise. Effects are bigger where vacant land exists, small where accessibility is already high, and weak in shrinking economies such as Detroit or where land is zoned for uses nobody wants.

A.5Reading: Glaeser, The Economics Approach to Cities

A.5.1Spatial equilibrium: no free lunchesGlaeser

The theoretical centerpiece. Workers, employers and builders each reach an equilibrium. For workers, income + amenities − housing costs − transport costs is the same across space, so a good thing in one place (high wages, easy access) is offset by a bad thing (high housing prices).

Memory tip: no free lunch: you cannot gain in one place without paying somewhere else.

A.5.2Alonso-Muth-Mills in GlaeserTerm listGlaeser

Within a metro area with income and amenities held constant, housing costs fall as transport costs rise with distance to the center. Evidence from Boston-area towns: prices and densities fall with distance from downtown, though other factors such as school quality can matter more for prices. A walk-or-drive choice (a car has a fixed cost) and decentralized employment both flatten the price slope. Access to public transport seems to explain why the poor live in city centers (Glaeser, Kahn and Rappaport); an alternative explanation is that the rich want more land.

A.5.3Rosen-Roback in GlaeserTerm listGlaeser

Across metro areas, high housing prices must reflect high income, high amenities, or both. People accept lower real income for warmer places, and high-income places tend to have offsetting negatives such as longer commutes. The predictions are somewhat fuzzy because income differences also reflect human capital.

A.5.4Employers: high wages need high productivityGlaeser

Firms pay high wages only where they gain something in return. Sources of productivity include natural advantages (a port or a resource), closeness to suppliers and customers, and, as goods transport got cheaper, face-to-face services such as law and finance. Closeness also speeds the exchange of ideas: Marshall's "mysteries of the trade in the air" and Jacobs's idea that new ideas combine old ones. Places with more educated people have higher wages and grow faster (human capital spillovers). The search for these sources is the study of agglomeration economies.

A.5.5Builders: why supply differsGlaeser

The builder's equilibrium asks why some places have abundant construction and low prices while others have little construction and high prices.

A.5.6Policy: people over placesGlaeser

Policies should be judged by whether they increase people's choices, not by whether they rebuild a place. Policy must anticipate that people and firms move, and governments only imperfectly represent their constituents.

Memory tip: Mono = one center (Chicago). Poly = many (LA, like a polygon). LA grew after highways, so it spread out like Broadacre.

A.6.2Chicago: lock-in and inertia (Cronon)Slides 3a

Why Chicago? Its factors lined up first, giving it lock-in and inertia. It became the primate city. The factors were:

  • Westward expansion and land speculation.
  • Railroads and Lake Michigan/river.
  • Engineering: raising the city and reversing the river.
  • Credit networks and machine politics.
  • Accidents: the fire and the World's Fair.

Cronon: it could have been another Midwest city such as St. Louis, but Chicago's factors lined up first.

A.6.3Machine CityTerm listSlides 3a

Can planners recreate Chicago? Networks, engineering and design: yes. Innovations, historic accidents and being first: no.

A.6.4Los Angeles: growth machineSlides 3a

Why LA? Its growth came from the growth machine (developers needed continual growth), aerospace/oil/freight/entertainment agglomeration, a shipping hub, engineered water, air conditioning, and "drive until you qualify" highways.

A.7Urbanization and migration (Jedwab & Vollrath)

A.7.1UrbanizationJedwab

Urbanization = share of people living in cities.

A.7.2Urbanization without GrowthTerm listJedwab

At the same income (GDP per capita), countries are 25–30 points more urban in 2010 than in 1500. This is Urbanization without Growth.

Four reasons explain it:

  1. The city got safer or easier to live in.
  2. Government policy favors cities (urban bias).
  3. The countryside got worse (rural push).
  4. More people survive in cities: births exceed deaths.
Memory tip: the four reasons spell S-U-R-B: Safer city, Urban bias, Rural push, Births exceed deaths. It sounds like "suburb".

A.7.3Rural push and urban pullTerm listJedwab

Rural push means the countryside gets worse (hardship, drought, war, no farmland). Urban pull is the opposite: jobs and wages. The lesson: moving to a city does not tell you that people became richer.

A.8Growth, decline and the changing economy

A.8.1Malthusian vs Solowian growthTerm list

Malthusian growth means population growth holds living standards flat. In Jedwab's reading, crowded cities raise death rates and balance wages. Solowian growth is sustained growth in which income per person rises through capital and technology (growth study guide).

Memory tip: Malthus = mouths: more mouths hold living standards flat. Solow = soars: income soars with capital and technology.

A.8.2Consumption City (Jedwab; Glaeser, Kolko and Saiz)Term listJedwab

Consumption City has two senses.

  • In Jedwab, resource windfalls such as oil, gold or diamonds are spent on urban goods and services. The workforce ends up in non-tradable services instead of tradable goods, so these cities miss the long-run growth effects of agricultural or industrial revolutions.
  • In Glaeser, Kolko and Saiz, cities succeed as places to consume: restaurants, entertainment, geography. Demand to live in cities has risen, high-amenity cities grew faster, and rents rose faster than wages (paper).

Week 3 (Jedwab) suggests the first sense.

Memory tip: Jedwab = a windfall that gets spent (no growth). Glaeser = amenities: a nice place to consume.

A.8.3Urban Economic TransitionTerm listJedwab

Developed countries deindustrialize and specialize in tradable services such as finance and business services, so manufacturing rises and then falls (Jedwab). Cities built on one industry suffer most, such as Detroit (autos).

Memory tip: factory to finance: manufacturing rises, then falls.

A.8.4Shrinking CitiesTerm list

Urban areas with falling population, economic activity and social vitality. Causes differ: deindustrialization, suburbanization and redlining, aging. Detroit is a standard example. Links to Grengs (Detroit) (Shrinking city, Detroit).

BWhat shape does the city take?

B.1Sprawl dimensions (Galster)

Galster separates a physical pattern from its causes and effects: causes → pattern → consequences. Eight dimensions describe the pattern:

B.1.1DensityTerm listGalster

Development per land area. Alone, it can't describe sprawl.

B.1.2ContinuityGalster

Unbroken development vs "leapfrog" gaps.

B.1.3ConcentrationGalster

Whether most development occupies a small share of the area.

B.1.4ClusteringGalster

Whether development within an area is tightly bunched.

B.1.5CentralityGalster

Closeness to the CBD.

B.1.6NuclearityGalster

One dominant center (mononuclear) vs several.

B.1.7Mixed usesGalster

Different activities in the same small area.

B.1.8ProximityGalster

How far apart different uses are across the city.

Memory tip: amount → gaps → spread → centers → uses is the order: Density, Continuity, Concentration + Clustering, Centrality + Nuclearity, Mixed uses + Proximity. Confusable pairs: Concentration = across the whole city, Clustering = inside one area. Centrality = how far from downtown, Nuclearity = how many downtowns. Mixed uses = same block, Proximity = same city.

B.1.9Why one word is not enoughGalster

Cities don't score consistently across these dimensions. The single word "sprawl" therefore hides a lot.

B.2Street networks and travel

B.2.1Griddedness (Boeing)Term listBoeing

Griddedness = orientation order + straightness + four-way intersections.

Street patterns changed in this order:

  • Connected grids first.
  • Then disconnected suburbs: loops, cul-de-sacs, and car-oriented streets, encouraged by finance and engineering rules.
  • Then a partial return toward grids in recent decades.

More grid-like neighborhoods have lower car ownership. This is an association, not proof. Street networks last for generations, so planners should build in connections.

Memory tip: picture a checkerboard: same direction (orientation order), straight lines, four-way crossings (+). Not T-junctions, not dead ends.

B.2.2The "D variables" (Ewing & Cervero)Term list

The "D variables" are density, diversity (land-use mix) and design (street network, for example intersection density and the share of four-way intersections). Later work extended them to 5 Ds by adding destination accessibility and distance to transit.

Ewing & Cervero found that driving is most related to destination accessibility, then street design; density matters weakly once the others are controlled. Boeing cites "Ds: density, diversity, design" (Ewing & Cervero).

Memory tip: the 3 Ds describe what is in the place (Density, Diversity, Design). The 2 added Ds are about getting there (Destination accessibility, Distance to transit).

B.3Outward movement and city experience

B.3.1SuburbanizationTerm listIhlanfeldt

Suburbanization = people and jobs moving to the periphery (job decentralization).

B.3.2Pedestrian vs auto-orientation: LA vs ChicagoTerm listSlides 3a

LA vs Chicago differ in pedestrian vs auto-orientation, the experience of density, affordability, and legibility.

CWho gets what? (urban problems)

C.1Jobs and unequal access

C.1.1Jobs-Housing Mismatch / spatial mismatch (Ihlanfeldt, Atlanta)Term listIhlanfeldt

Jobs move to the suburbs, but housing discrimination keeps Black workers from following. Their choices shrink.

Barriers include not hearing about jobs, no usable transport, and hiring discrimination. Harms include unemployment, lower wages, and higher commuting cost (lower net earnings).

Count suitable jobs and competing workers. Policy can move people to jobs, move jobs to people, or improve connections through job information and transport.

C.1.2Modal Mismatch (Grengs, Detroit)Term listGrengs

The same neighborhood offers good job access by car and very poor access by transit. Mobility ≠ accessibility. A neighborhood average hides this difference (ecological fallacy).

The gravity model gives nearer jobs more weight and adjusts for competing workers. Disadvantaged residents also make chained trips (home, childcare, work, shops), which a simple home-to-work bus route may not serve (trip-chaining). This is the idea behind cumulative/competitive access.

Memory tip: spatial = space between homes and jobs (Ihlanfeldt). Modal = mode of travel, car versus bus (Grengs).

C.2Accessibility measures

C.2.1Primal vs dual accessibilityTerm list

Primal counts the opportunities reachable within a time limit (jobs within 20 minutes). Dual finds the travel time needed to reach a set number of opportunities (time to reach 2 jobs). This comes from a search summary only, so check the class wording (source).

Memory tip: primal = piles (count what you can reach in 20 minutes). Dual = duration (how long to reach 2 jobs).

C.2.2Cumulative vs competitive accessibility (Shen)Term list

Cumulative counts opportunities within a travel-time threshold and ignores that reaching them gets harder as time grows. Competitive (Shen) divides reachable jobs by the people competing for them. Grengs's gravity model adjusts for competition (Shen 1998 discussion).

Memory tip: cumulative = count. Competitive = count ÷ competitors.

C.3Travel capacity and street management

C.3.1Travel supply and demandTerm list

Supply is road and transit capacity; demand is people's travel.

C.3.2Induced demand (Dexter)Dexter

Induced demand means added road capacity attracts new driving, so congestion returns. Dexter: more congestion lowers accessibility, and adding infrastructure has limits (induced demand).

C.3.3Congestion pricingTerm list

Charging drivers to use congested roads or enter congested areas (cordon or area pricing, or tolls on a single facility). It cuts demand instead of adding supply (congestion pricing).

Memory tip: induced demand: more lanes, more cars. Congestion pricing: fewer cars, by price.

C.3.4Traffic calmingTerm list

Street-design measures such as speed humps, narrower lanes and pedestrian refuges slow cars and make walking and biking safer. Unlike signs and signals, they change the street itself. Links to pedestrian-oriented streets (B) and Gehl (E) (FHWA primer).

Memory tip: calm the street, not the sign: change the shape of the road.

C.4Health and environmental burdens

C.4.1WilsonWilson

Zoning and planning decide who gets healthy surroundings. Exclusionary zoning keeps poorer families out of better towns. Metropolitan fragmentation means no one ensures fairness across the region.

C.4.2Riskscape and sedentary habitsTerm listWilson

Burdens pile up as a "riskscape": food, unsafe streets, stress, pollution, and sedentary habits. This is an environmental injustice. Redevelopment must let existing residents stay.

C.4.3Extreme weather eventsTerm listSlides 6a

Cities are sinking as seas rise. Extreme weather events overload drains because pavement replaces grass.

C.4.4Heat islandsTerm listSlides 6a

Heat islands come from concrete, steel and lack of shade. Air conditioning strains grids.

C.5Housing supply and household change

C.5.1Shortage / surplusTerm listSlides 6a

Supply = housing units; demand = population. Demand is inelastic because everyone needs a home.

But it can adjust through migration (Rosen-Roback), subdividing, households combining or splitting, submarkets, and location (jobs-housing balance). Measures include vacancy, new construction, household formation, and affordability (rent-to-income).

C.5.2Life courseSlides 6a

Young people move to cities. Around 30, people form families and need a home. Older people leave.

C.5.3FilteringTerm listSlides 6a

Houses get older and filter down, while people filter up. A high-quality home for a 40-year-old becomes a starter home for a 30-year-old. Old buildings = affordable stock.

Memory tip: houses sink, people climb.

C.5.4Building regulationTerm list

Local rules on what and how much can be built, such as zoning use categories, minimum lot sizes, setbacks, height, floor-area ratio, parking and building codes. Restrictive rules limit housing supply. Links: Wilson (exclusionary zoning), NIMBYism, housing shortage (Bipartisan Policy Center).

C.6Housing inequality and social contact

C.6.1SegregationSlides 6a

Housing is the main mechanism, through lending discrimination and HOAs. Entropy and dissimilarity indices measure it.

C.6.2GentrificationTerm listSlides 6a

Wealthier inflow → displacement, gentrification-in-place, cultural erasure. It is a "wicked problem": improving a neighborhood can push out the poor.

Memory tip: Out, Squeezed, Erased = displacement, gentrification-in-place, cultural erasure.

C.6.3NIMBYism and fixesTerm listSlides 6a

Resistance can become NIMBYism ("not in my backyard") and NIABY, leaving no development anywhere. Fixes include mixed-rent housing and participatory planning.

C.6.4Contact Hypothesis (Valentine)Term listValentine

Contact improves relations, but not when groups compete. In Great Depression subdivided homes, new nonwhite residents were blamed for falling quality.

Valentine: proximity ≠ interaction; politeness ≠ changed beliefs; tolerance ≠ respect. Good experiences become "exceptions". Micro-publics (shared activities) may help. Meaningful contact must scale up.

Memory tip: contact helps unless the groups compete. Valentine's three "not the same": proximity ≠ interaction, politeness ≠ changed beliefs, tolerance ≠ respect.
Stop and recall: Explain spatial mismatch vs modal mismatch in one sentence each. What is housing "filtering"? What are three outcomes of gentrification? What does Valentine say about contact? Primal vs dual and cumulative vs competitive accessibility. Induced demand and how congestion pricing answers it. Why does traffic calming matter for walking?

DHow did planners respond? (planning movements)

D.1Industrial crises and early responses

D.1.1Planning after crises: Industrial Revolution (1800s)Slides 6a

Pattern: planning follows crises. The Industrial Revolution (1800s) brought fast urbanization with no governance (laissez-faire). Overcrowding, disease, and inequality followed. Two responses emerged: utopian movements ("solve everything at once") and technical officials ("one problem at a time").

Memory tip: the chain in seven words: Sick → Pretty → Green → Rigid → Human → Walkable → Tiny = Sanitary, City Beautiful, Garden City, Modernism, Jacobs, New Urbanism and TOD, Tactical Urbanism. Each one fixes the last one's flaw.

D.1.2Sanitary MovementTerm listSlides 6a

Responded to overcrowding and disease through public health. It was the origin of zoning for health.

D.1.3City Beautiful (early 1900s US; Burnham, Robinson, Olmsted)Term listSlides 6a

Emphasized beauty, cleanliness, leisure space, and park-and-boulevard systems. It brought the first large-scale modern planning and municipal bureaucracy.

Critics called it surface aesthetics and top-down. It ignored housing and population and was short-lived. Its legacy includes parks, Beaux-Arts buildings, and municipal power.

D.2Decentralized towns and neighborhoods

D.2.1Garden City (late 1800s England; Howard, Unwin, Parker)Term listSlides 6a

Proposed decentralized, small self-sufficient towns with green belts and communal land. It emphasized social reform more than City Beautiful. But Letchworth shifted to residential design, so the principles went into suburbs.

Memory tip: Beautiful = Boulevards and Beaux-Arts (looks). Garden = Green belts and small towns (living).

D.2.2Neighborhood Unit (Clarence Perry)Term listSlides 6a

Clarence Perry's Neighborhood Unit appears in the Garden City part of the slides. The slides give the name only, with no definition.

D.3Modernist order and its alternative

D.3.1Modernism (1920s on)Term listSlides 6a

Favored top-down, rational, ornament-free, geometric order, single-use zones, and the same approach everywhere.

D.3.2Le Corbusier's Radiant CityTerm listSlides 6a

"get people off the streets into parks". After 1950, it meant highways, clearance, urban renewal, public housing, and car dependence.

D.3.3Frank Lloyd Wright's Broadacre CityTerm listSlides 6a

The alternative was Frank Lloyd Wright's Broadacre City: decentralized, an acre per family, car-based, nature woven in, and infinitely repeatable.

Memory tip: Radiant = rise: towers in parks. Broadacre = broad: flat, spread out, an acre each. LA looks like Broadacre.

D.4Street life and human-scale planning

D.4.1Jane Jacobs (The Death and Life of Great American Cities)Term listJacobs

Opposed Modernism and Robert Moses (Lower Manhattan Expressway). Street life is complicated order, not chaos: sidewalk ballet, eyes on the street.

D.4.2Informal public supervisionJacobs

The Week 5 reading explains safety through informal public supervision. It requires three conditions: clear public/private boundaries, eyes on the street, and fairly continuous sidewalk use. Shops add four things: walkers, a watcher with a stake in the street, something worth watching, and attention.

Memory tip: three conditions = B-E-H: Boundary between public and private, Eyes on the street, Hours of continuous use.

D.4.3Urban VitalityTerm listSlides 7a

Street activity or a built environment that supports it. The mechanism is built environment → mixed population → active all day → sidewalk contacts → safety and cohesion.

Its six dimensions are density, small blocks, mixed primary uses, aged buildings, accessibility, and no border vacuums.

Memory tip: six conditions = People, Paths, Purposes, Prices, Arrival, Interruptions: density, small blocks, mixed uses, aged buildings, accessibility, no border vacuums.

D.4.4Critiques of JacobsSlides 7a

Critiques of Jacobs:

  • NIMBYism and gentrification.
  • Hard to measure.
  • Jacobs described what happened naturally; it can't simply be copied (Alexander, "A City is Not a Tree").
  • Mixed empirical results: more walking, but maybe only necessary walking; mixed use raises social cohesion, while density lowers it.

D.5Walkable development and small interventions

D.5.1New Urbanism (Duany & Plater-Zyberk)Slides 7a

Emphasizes walkability, connectivity, mixed use, density, green space, and traditional neighborhood design (Seaside, Florida).

D.5.2Transit-Oriented Development (Calthorpe)Term listSlides 7a

Dense mixed use and short blocks within ~10 minutes of stations.

D.5.3Urban Acupuncture (Lerner, Curitiba) / Tactical UrbanismTerm listSlides 7a

Uses small, cheap, temporary interventions (guerrilla, DIY, pop-up) instead of master plans. It follows Lynch.

Stop and recall (draw it): Write the planning chain from Sanitary to Tactical Urbanism on paper. Then add who opposed whom. For Modernism and Jacobs, give one city-scale and one human-scale effect.

EHow do people feel the city?

E.1Public space thinkers

E.1.1Jan Gehl: necessary, optional and resultant activitiesTerm listSlides 7a

Distinguishes necessary / optional / resultant activities. Poor spaces get only necessary activities; good spaces get lingering and social activities. Low-intensity contacts matter.

Auto-oriented streets kill optional activity. Gehl is not sure about grids and is skeptical of extreme density.

Memory tip: N-O-R: Necessary, Optional, Resultant. Need → option → result. Bad spaces only get "need".

E.1.2Kevin Lynch: the city as a learning ecologyTerm listSlides 7a

Sees the city as a learning ecology: provide a good space and let people improve it. The dimensions are vitality, sense (legibility), fit, access, control, efficiency, and justice. The approach is bottom-up, not comprehensive.

Memory tip: VSF ACE J: Vitality, Sense, Fit, Access, Control, Efficiency, Justice.

E.1.3William WhyteTerm listSlides 7a

Spent ten years watching NYC plazas. People attract people; effective capacity; people use spaces their own way. Seating and food matter, and groups choose good places.

Keeping out "undesirables" also keeps out everyone (hostile architecture).

E.1.4Triangulation (Whyte)Term listSlides 7a

Strangers talk when both look at the same thing: an external stimulus such as a sculpture, a street performer, or even two bank robbers being caught.

Memory tip: a triangle: two strangers plus a third thing to look at.

E.1.5Ray Oldenburg: third placesTerm listSlides 7a

third places are neutral, leveling, conversation-centered, accessible, low profile, playful, and at-home, with regulars. Oldenburg cares more about a place's character and regulars than foot traffic.

Memory tip: home is the first place, work the second, and a third place is everyone's living room. Group the eight traits: come and go (Neutral, Accessible), treated alike (Leveling, Playful), what happens (Conversation, Regulars), how it feels (Low profile, At-home).

E.2Whyte's film: The Social Life of Small Urban Spaces

These notes come from the film on the video list and from the class discussion. The film's captions are auto-generated, so check names against the video.

Memory tip: the film's design basics are S-S-S-W-T-F: Sitting, Street link, Sun and light, Water, Trees, Food.

E.2.1The problem is underuseVideo

Whyte's team started out worried about overcrowding and found the opposite. The great problem of these spaces is underuse. Most office-building plazas were empty most of the time, even at lunch on a beautiful day. The city gave builders floor-space bonuses for plazas, so builders added extra floors and gave back empty plazas.

E.2.2People-watching and sociabilityVideo

The number one activity is people looking at other people. The most used plazas have more people in twos and threes, and also the greatest number of people overall. A busy place is the most congenial kind of place.

E.2.3Edges, steps and cornersVideo

People rarely stop to talk in the middle of a large space. They look for steps, ledges and edges. The corners of steps are busy walkways, yet people stand and sit there anyway: a friendly kind of congestion. Street corners work the same way for chance meetings.

E.2.4Sitting and choiceVideo

The main finding is that people sit where sitting is possible and comfortable, so the first rule is suitable seating. Ledges, steps and grass give choice. Fussy railings, spikes and isolated benches are socially awkward. Movable chairs give users control over where they sit and how close they sit to others, and moving chairs is a social ritual.

E.2.5Effective capacityVideo

Capacity is self-leveling. At peak lunch, with heavy turnover, the number of people at a ledge stays nearly constant even though there is room for more. People sense the number that is right for a place. The most crowded small parks (Paley and Greenacre) are perceived as quiet because people have choices and comfortable social distances.

E.2.6The relationship to the streetVideo

The most important thing about a space is its relationship to the street. Paley Park is not a refuge. It is an intensely urban place, and passers-by look in and smile. A few easy steps from the street draw people in; New York's new zoning requires plazas to be no more than three feet above or below street level. Self-contained megastructures such as Houston Center and Renaissance Center reject the street. In the class discussion, Dealey Plaza works because it is visible from the street ("see and be seen") while a slight height difference separates it from traffic.

E.2.7Safety, "undesirables" and who keeps orderVideo

"Undesirables" are mostly harmless, and spikes or short benches meant to keep them out keep everyone out (hostile architecture). Odd people reassure us of our own normality. Bryant Park is dangerous because fences and walls cut it off from the street, which creates a feeling of entrapment. Cameras are mostly symbolic; a human presence works better, like a "mayor" of the plaza. In the class discussion, food vendors act as informal "sheriffs".

E.2.8Sun, water, trees and foodVideo

Sun matters less than expected: it matters most in cold weather, and light and wind shelter matter too. Water should be touchable and usable, not fenced off, and its sound masks traffic noise. Trees in groves make a slightly recessed, protected feel. Food seeds activity: vendors and cafés attract more people and become rendezvous points.

E.2.9Scale and critical massVideo

The basics are the same from city to city; the key variable is scale. Low-density cities need to concentrate activity to reach a critical mass, ideally in the center of town (Fountain Square in Cincinnati).

E.2.10What New York's zoning changedVideo

Whyte's recommendations went into New York's zoning: sitting space, a close link with the street, trees, food (encouraged, not required), at least half of building frontage for retail, access for disabled people, and maintenance rules.

E.3Urban sociology and social relations

E.3.1Georg Simmel: the blasé attitude and the protective organTerm listSlides 7a

Stimulation overload → blasé attitude as a "protective organ".

E.3.2Louis Wirth: collective personalityTerm listSlides 7a

Size, density, heterogeneity → a collective personality.

E.3.3Erving Goffman: civil inattentionTerm listSlides 7a

The interaction order includes civil inattention: a nod, brief apology, or looking away. Lofland calls it politeness, not coldness. Morrill: "together alone".

Memory tip: Simmel = self-protection (blasé). Wirth = whole-city personality. Goffman = greeting rituals (nod, apology, look away).

E.3.4Putnam, "Bowling Alone"Term listSlides 7a

Social capital fell. Aelbrecht: new spaces emerged, including malls and gas stations.

Memory tip: Putnam says community is gone. Aelbrecht says it moved, to malls and gas stations.

E.3.5Loneliness vs solitudeSlides 7a

Contacts and loneliness are weakly related. Solitude means being comfortable alone.

E.4Urban lives and shared meaning (Borer)

E.4.1Urbanism vs urbanization (Borer)Borer

Urbanism means urban lives and culture; urbanization means growth. Sensing is social.

E.4.2Locational socializationBorer
