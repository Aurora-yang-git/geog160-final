A. Why a city exists, and where

What counts as a city?

There is no single definition of a city. Choose the definition that fits your question:

  • Administrative: a government's territory.
  • Physical: the built-up area.
  • Population: population density.
  • Function: the area connected by commuting, trade, and services.
  • Culture/experience: a shared sense of place.

Functional and Tributary Areas means defining a city by what it does. These are the areas that "belong" to it: its commuting zone, TV market, sports fans, or medical service area.

The core idea: land price versus travel cost

A city's pattern comes from millions of small decisions, such as where to open a firm or rent a home. Each person maximizes "utility"—money, time, and enjoyment—by making trade-offs.

  • Central locations have the highest land price and lowest travel cost.
  • Outer locations have the lowest land price and highest travel cost.

The system settles into a balance, or equilibrium. Every theory below applies this idea.

Chicago vs Los Angeles (Week 3)

Chicago is Monocentric, with one center. Los Angeles is Polycentric, with several centers.

Why Chicago? Its factors lined up first, giving it lock-in and inertia. It became the primate city. The factors were:

  • Westward expansion and land speculation.
  • Railroads and Lake Michigan/river.
  • Engineering: raising the city and reversing the river.
  • Credit networks and machine politics.
  • Accidents: the fire and the World's Fair.

Cronon: it could have been Minneapolis, St. Louis, Milwaukee or Detroit, but Chicago's factors lined up first.

Machine City: can planners recreate Chicago? Networks, engineering and design: yes. Innovations, historic accidents and being first: no.

Why LA? Its growth came from the growth machine (developers needed continual growth), aerospace/oil/freight/entertainment agglomeration, a shipping hub, engineered water, air conditioning, and "drive until you qualify" highways.

Why people move to cities (Week 3, Jedwab & Vollrath)

Urbanization = share of people living in cities. At the same income, countries are 25–30 points more urban in 2010 than in 1500. This is Urbanization without Growth.

Four reasons explain it:

  1. The city got safer or easier to live in.
  2. Government policy favors cities (urban bias).
  3. The countryside got worse (rural push).
  4. More people survive in cities: births exceed deaths.

Urban pull is the opposite: jobs and wages. The lesson: moving to a city does not tell you that people became richer.

Stop and recall: (1) Compare Chicago vs LA: centers, density, when built. (2) Why Chicago? (3) What does "urbanization without growth" mean, and what are the four reasons? (4) What does Machine City ask?

B. What shape does the city take?

Galster: eight dimensions of sprawl

Galster separates a physical pattern from its causes and effects: causes → pattern → consequences. Eight dimensions describe the pattern:

  1. Density: development per land area. Alone, it can't describe sprawl.
  2. Continuity: unbroken development vs "leapfrog" gaps.
  3. Concentration: whether most development occupies a small share of the area.
  4. Clustering: whether development within an area is tightly bunched.
  5. Centrality: closeness to the CBD.
  6. Nuclearity: one dominant center (mononuclear) vs several.
  7. Mixed uses: different activities in the same small area.
  8. Proximity: how far apart different uses are across the city.

Cities don't score consistently across these dimensions, as tested on 13 urbanized areas. The single word "sprawl" therefore hides a lot.

Boeing: streets and griddedness

Griddedness = orientation order + straightness + four-way intersections.

Street patterns changed in this order:

  • Connected grids (pre-1940).
  • Disconnected suburbs: loops, cul-de-sacs, and car-oriented streets, encouraged by finance and engineering rules.
  • A partial return after 2000.

More grid-like neighborhoods have lower car ownership. This is an association, not proof. Street networks last for generations, so planners should build in connections.

Moving outward

Suburbanization = people and jobs moving to the periphery (job decentralization).

Experiencing city form

LA vs Chicago differ in pedestrian vs auto-orientation, the experience of density, affordability, and legibility.

Stop and recall: List Galster's eight dimensions from memory. Which two are easy to confuse (concentration/clustering; centrality/nuclearity; mixed uses/proximity)? What three features make up griddedness?

C. Who gets what? (urban problems)

Jobs (Week 4)

  • Jobs-Housing Mismatch / spatial mismatch (Ihlanfeldt, Atlanta): jobs move to the suburbs, but housing discrimination keeps Black workers from following. Their choices shrink. Barriers include not hearing about jobs, no usable transport, and hiring discrimination. Harms include unemployment, lower wages, and higher commuting cost (lower net earnings). Count suitable jobs and competing workers. Policy can move people to jobs, move jobs to people, or improve connections through job information and transport.
  • Modal Mismatch (Grengs, Detroit): the same neighborhood offers good job access by car and very poor access by transit. Mobility ≠ accessibility. A neighborhood average hides this difference (ecological fallacy). The gravity model gives nearer jobs more weight and adjusts for competing workers. This is the idea behind cumulative/competitive access.

Health and the environment

  • Wilson (Week 3): zoning and planning decide who gets healthy surroundings. Exclusionary zoning keeps poorer families out of better towns. Metropolitan fragmentation means no one ensures fairness across the region. Burdens pile up as a "riskscape": food, unsafe streets, stress, pollution, and sedentary habits. This is an environmental injustice. Redevelopment must let existing residents stay.
  • Environment (6a): cities are sinking as seas rise. Extreme weather events overload drains because pavement replaces grass. Heat islands come from concrete, steel and lack of shade. Air conditioning strains grids.

Housing (6a)

  • Shortage / surplus: supply = housing units; demand = population. Demand is inelastic because everyone needs a home. But it can adjust through migration (Rosen-Roback), subdividing, households combining or splitting, submarkets, and location (jobs-housing balance). Measures include vacancy, new construction, household formation, and affordability (rent-to-income).
  • Life course: young people move to cities. Around 30, people form families and need a home. Older people leave.
  • Filtering: houses get older and filter down, while people filter up. A high-quality home for a 40-year-old becomes a starter home for a 30-year-old. Old buildings = affordable stock.
  • Segregation: housing is the main mechanism, through lending discrimination and HOAs. Entropy and dissimilarity indices measure it.
  • Gentrification: wealthier inflow → displacement, gentrification-in-place, cultural erasure. It is a "wicked problem": improving a neighborhood can push out the poor. Resistance can become NIMBYism ("not in my backyard") and NIABY, leaving no development anywhere. Fixes include mixed-rent housing and participatory planning.
  • Contact Hypothesis: contact improves relations, but not when groups compete. In Great Depression subdivided homes, new nonwhite residents were blamed for falling quality. Valentine (Week 5): proximity ≠ interaction; politeness ≠ changed beliefs; tolerance ≠ respect. Good experiences become "exceptions". Micro-publics (shared activities) may help. Meaningful contact must scale up.
Stop and recall: Explain spatial mismatch vs modal mismatch in one sentence each. What is housing "filtering"? What are three outcomes of gentrification? What does Valentine say about contact?

D. How did planners respond? (planning movements)

Pattern: planning follows crises. The Industrial Revolution (1800s) brought fast urbanization with no governance (laissez-faire). Overcrowding, disease, and inequality followed. Two responses emerged: utopian movements ("solve everything at once") and technical officials ("one problem at a time").

  1. Sanitary Movement: responded to overcrowding and disease through public health. It was the origin of zoning for health.
  2. City Beautiful (early 1900s US; Burnham, Robinson, Olmsted): emphasized beauty, cleanliness, leisure space, and park-and-boulevard systems. It brought the first large-scale modern planning and municipal bureaucracy. Critics called it surface aesthetics and top-down. It ignored housing and population and was short-lived. Its legacy includes parks, Beaux-Arts buildings, and municipal power.
  3. Garden City (late 1800s England; Howard, Unwin, Parker): proposed decentralized, small self-sufficient towns with green belts and communal land. It emphasized social reform more than City Beautiful. But Letchworth shifted to residential design, so the principles went into suburbs. Neighborhood Unit (Clarence Perry) appears in this section.
  4. Modernism (1920s on): favored top-down, rational, ornament-free, geometric order, single-use zones, and the same approach everywhere. Le Corbusier's Radiant City (1935): "get people off the streets into parks". After 1950, it meant highways, clearance, urban renewal, public housing, and car dependence. The alternative was Frank Lloyd Wright's Broadacre City (1932–35): decentralized, an acre per family, car-based, nature woven in, and infinitely repeatable.
  5. Jane Jacobs (1961, Death and Life of Great American Cities): opposed Modernism and Robert Moses (Lower Manhattan Expressway). Street life is complicated order, not chaos: sidewalk ballet, eyes on the street. The Week 5 reading explains safety through informal public supervision. It requires three conditions: clear public/private boundaries, eyes on the street, and fairly continuous sidewalk use. Shops add walkers, watchers, and attention.
  6. Urban Vitality: street activity or a built environment that supports it. The mechanism is built environment → mixed population → active all day → sidewalk contacts → safety and cohesion. Its six dimensions are density, small blocks, mixed primary uses, aged buildings, accessibility, and no border vacuums.
  7. Gehl, Lynch, Whyte, Oldenburg: human-scale thinkers; see E.
  8. New Urbanism (Duany & Plater-Zyberk, 1991): emphasizes walkability, connectivity, mixed use, density, green space, and traditional neighborhood design (Seaside, Florida).
  9. Transit-Oriented Development (Calthorpe, 1993): dense mixed use and short blocks within ~10 minutes of stations.
  10. Urban Acupuncture (Lerner, Curitiba) / Tactical Urbanism: uses small, cheap, temporary interventions (guerrilla, DIY, pop-up) instead of master plans. It follows Lynch.

Critiques of Jacobs:

  • NIMBYism and gentrification.
  • Hard to measure.
  • Jacobs described what happened naturally; it can't simply be copied (Alexander, "A City is Not a Tree").
  • Mixed empirical results: more walking, but maybe only necessary walking; mixed use raises social cohesion, while density lowers it.
Stop and recall (draw it): Write the planning chain from Sanitary to Tactical Urbanism on paper. Then add who opposed whom. For Modernism and Jacobs, give one city-scale and one human-scale effect.

E. How do people feel the city?

Public space thinkers (7a)

  • Jan Gehl (1971): distinguishes necessary / optional / resultant activities. Poor spaces get only necessary activities; good spaces get lingering and social activities. Low-intensity contacts matter. Auto-oriented streets kill optional activity. Gehl is not sure about grids and is skeptical of extreme density.
  • Kevin Lynch (1981): sees the city as a learning ecology: provide a good space and let people improve it. The dimensions are vitality, sense (legibility), fit, access, control, efficiency, and justice. The approach is bottom-up, not comprehensive.
  • William Whyte (1980): spent ten years watching NYC plazas. People attract people; effective capacity; people use spaces their own way. Seating and food matter, and groups choose good places. Triangulation: strangers talk when both look at the same thing. Keeping out "undesirables" also keeps out everyone (hostile architecture).
  • Ray Oldenburg (1989): third places are neutral, leveling, conversation-centered, accessible, low profile, playful, and at-home, with regulars. Oldenburg cares more about a place's character and regulars than foot traffic.

Urban sociology

  • Simmel (1903): stimulation overload → blasé attitude as a "protective organ".
  • Wirth (1938): size, density, heterogeneity → a collective personality.
  • Goffman: the interaction order includes civil inattention: a nod, brief apology, or looking away. Lofland calls it politeness, not coldness. Morrill: "together alone".
  • Putnam, "Bowling Alone" (2000): social capital fell. Aelbrecht: new spaces emerged, including malls and gas stations.
  • Loneliness vs solitude: contacts and loneliness are weakly related. Solitude means being comfortable alone.

Borer (Week 6): experiencing the city

Urbanism means urban lives and culture; urbanization means growth. Sensing is social. Locational socialization: shared activity gives a place its meaning.

Three ways into this idea:

  • Simmel: blasé.
  • Benjamin: the flâneur, a stroller reading the street.
  • Goffman: face-saving and interaction order.

Five sensescapes describe the experience:

  • Sight: landmarks and readability. Gans's West End was misread as a slum and demolished.
  • Sound: keynote, soundmarks, and sound signals. Noise depends on the listener.
  • Smell: cultural judgments, e.g. "stench of the poor".
  • Taste: Bourdieu: taste marks status; Gans: taste cultures; omnivores.
  • Touch: walking, parkour, and unequal freedom of movement.
Stop and recall: Name Gehl's three activities; explain Lynch's key contribution and Whyte's triangulation; compare Oldenburg vs Jacobs and Simmel vs Goffman; name Borer's five senses.

Reading one-liners (for essays)

  • Dexter: land use and transport shape each other. Glaeser: people, firms and builders respond to costs until places balance.
  • Galster: sprawl = eight dimensions of physical pattern. Boeing: streets left the grid and partly returned.
  • Jedwab & Vollrath: urbanization alone doesn't show growth. Wilson: planning and zoning decide who gets healthy surroundings.
  • Ihlanfeldt: segregation + suburban jobs. Grengs: car vs transit access in Detroit.
  • Jacobs: safe streets come from eyes and continuous use. Valentine: contact ≠ respect. Borer: the city is sensed and socially made.

City scale vs human scale (say this in every answer)

Show both scales in your answers:

  • City scale: planners solved big problems—disease, congestion, housing—with comprehensive plans.
  • Human scale: those plans often ignored daily experience, so Jacobs, Gehl, Whyte, Lynch pushed back.

The best answers show both.
