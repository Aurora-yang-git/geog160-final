AWhy a city exists, and where

A.1Definitions of a city

Define a city to fit the question:

  • Administrative: government territory.
  • Physical: built-up area.
  • Population: density.
  • Function: commuting, trade and service connections.
  • Culture/experience: shared sense of place.

A.1.1Functional and Tributary AreasTerm listSlides 1b

What Functional areas are linked by activities; tributary areas are served by an urban center.

How Commuting, shopping and medical trips connect places beyond city limits.

Effect Different activities produce different, overlapping boundaries.

Example / link Someone may live outside the city but work and shop inside it.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Word association

Tributaries feed a river; tributary areas connect to a center. Picture commute, shopping and hospital trips crossing a city boundary. Function follows flows, not the map’s outline.

Additional course detail

Define cities by functions and connected areas: commuting zones, TV markets, sports fans and medical service areas.

A.2.1Land price versus travel costSlides 2a

  • Center: highest land price, lowest travel cost; outskirts: reverse. All theories below use this trade-off.

A.2.3Von Thünen modelTerm listSlides 2a

What Von Thünen explains farming patterns around a market.

How Farmers compare land rent, production costs and transport costs.

Effect Different products can afford different locations, creating rings under the model’s assumptions.

Example / link A product costly to transport has a reason to locate near the market.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Picture

Picture farm rings around a market, each paying a delivery bill. Greater distance raises transport cost; different products can afford different rents. Rings come from competing bids.

Additional course detail

Von Thünen: first transport–land-use theory; farmers trade rent against market transport costs, anticipating urban economics.

  • Profit = selling price − production cost − transport cost.
  • Distance raises transport costs → heavy/profitable goods near market; light/land-hungry goods farther away.

A.2.4Central Place TheoryTerm listSlides 2a

What Central Place Theory explains why settlements form a service hierarchy.

How Everyday shops need a small customer area; specialized services need a larger one.

Effect Small centers are numerous; large centers are fewer and farther apart.

Example / link Convenience stores are common; major specialist hospitals are less common.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Everyday example

Milk nearby; a rare specialist farther away. Frequent, ordinary purchases sustain many small centers; specialized services need larger catchments and fewer centers.

Additional course detail

Central Place Theory: specialized goods attract fewer buyers traveling farther → few large, widely spaced centers; everyday groceries → numerous small, nearby towns.

  • Chicago complicates this hierarchy: it grew first; smaller cities followed.

A.2.5Sector and Multiple Nuclei modelsTerm listSlides 2a

What Hoyt’s Sector Model follows corridors; the Multiple Nuclei Model has several centers.

How Transport routes shape sectors, while related activities cluster into different centers.

Effect Cities need not grow as rings around one downtown.

Example / link Picture wedges along railways versus separate shopping, industrial and office centers.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Picture

Sector = pizza slices along roads. Multiple nuclei = several egg yolks in one pan. The first emphasizes corridors; the second emphasizes several activity centers.

Additional course detail
  • Hoyt: similar development follows rail/highway corridors, not straight-line distance.
  • Harris and Ullman: complementary uses attract; homes/factories repel → multiple centers rather than one CBD.
  • Single-CBD cities grow together → Multiple Nuclei better fits post-highway cities.

A.2.6Alonso-Muth-Mills model (intracity)Term listSlides 2a

What AMM explains housing choices within one city（城内）.

How People trade housing costs against commuting costs.

Effect Homes farther from the center usually offer cheaper space but longer trips.

Example / link Think: small downtown flat versus large suburban home. Rosen–Roback compares different cities.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Name cue

AMM = “A Metropolitan Market” as a memory cue: stay inside one city. Draw a seesaw—central rent rises as commuting cost falls.

Additional course detail

Alonso-Muth-Mills: everyone works in the CBD; households trade cheaper outer housing against longer commutes.

  • Distance → commuting costs rise → rent/density fall; predicts rents, density and building heights; extends to multiple CBDs.
  • Dexter: lower-income US households consume less housing to live centrally.

A.2.7Rosen-Roback model (intercity)Term listSlides 2a

What Rosen–Roback explains choices between cities（城际）.

How People weigh wages, rents and amenities: income, housing cost and quality of life.

Effect High wages may be offset by high rent.

Example / link Someone may accept lower pay to live near the beach. AMM stays within one city.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Sound cue

Rosen–Roback → ROAM. A worker roams between cities carrying three price tags: wage, rent, amenities. Contrast AMM, which stays inside one city.

Additional course detail

Rosen-Roback: free movement between metros balances wages, rents, productivity and amenities.

  • High rent + high wages → high productivity.
  • High rent + low wages → high amenities.
  • Low rent + low wages → low productivity or amenities.

A.3Clustering and transport change

A.3.1AgglomerationTerm listSlides 2a

What Agglomeration brings benefits from clustering people and firms.

How They share resources, find workers and exchange ideas more easily.

Effect Productivity may rise, but so can rents and congestion.

Example / link A design firm benefits from nearby clients and talent, while paying more for space.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Word association

Agglomeration → a GLOB of firms. Nearness pays through sharing resources, matching workers and exchanging ideas. Then add the bill: rent and congestion.

Additional course detail

Agglomeration: clustering lowers travel costs; specialization raises wages; knowledge spillovers increase productivity. Also cultural.

  • One innovation can displace others: Ford, Detroit.
Memory tip: three benefits: Cluster, Specialize, Spill over.

A.4Reading: Dexter, Transportation and Land Use

A.4.1Land use and transport shape each otherDexter

Dexter's duality: development determines travel needs; transport networks determine development locations.

Memory tip: two-way street.

A.4.2Cheaper transport pulls people and firms outwardDexter

Dexter: cheaper/faster transport draws people outward.

  • Expressways/rail → lower CBD density, higher demand for nearby land; faster CBD access can increase sprawl.

A.4.3Firms: Weber, central places and tradeTerm listDexter

Weber: firms minimize transport, labor and agglomeration costs; similar industries share resources/knowledge.

  • Trade theory: transport usually dominates location costs.
  • Cheaper transport + communications → efficient firms, complex global supply chains.
  • Goods transport cheaper than commuting → firms decentralize toward workers; time-sensitive sales → nearer customers.

A.4.4From the pedestrian era to the freeway eraTerm listDexter

Transport change shifted concentrated CBD employment toward faster suburban growth and polycentric cities.

  • Pedestrian/streetcar: CBD jobs; those able to afford moving left; streetcars sent all but the poorest outward.
  • Freeways: middle-class mobility → suburbs outgrow central cities; falling transit ridership → government takeover of many lines.
  • Sprawl → self-contained suburban sectors/downtowns; freeway intersections → employment centers; cheap peripheral land → logistics warehouses.

A.4.5Accessibility and congestionDexter

Dexter: accessibility = jobs reachable in typically 30 minutes or 1 hour.

  • Faster travel raises accessibility/desirability/land cost; congestion lowers accessibility.
  • Southern California: rising congestion linked to weaker output.

A.4.6Spatial mismatch and transitDexter

Dexter: spatial mismatch = poor accessibility; mixed use + mass transit may reduce vehicle miles and free parking space. US transit use is low, usually subsidized.

A.4.7Transport investment and land valueDexter

Dexter: transport investment, even announcements, raises property values/development.

  • Wider freeway → more traffic → congestion returns: "build it and they will come."
  • Effects: bigger with vacant land; small with already-high accessibility; weak in shrinking Detroit or unwanted zoned uses.

A.5Reading: Glaeser, The Economics Approach to Cities

A.5.1Spatial equilibrium: no free lunchesGlaeser

Glaeser's centerpiece: workers, employers and builders reach spatial equilibrium.

  • Workers: income + amenities − housing costs − transport costs equal across space; high wages/easy access offset by high housing prices.
Memory tip: no free lunch.

A.5.2Alonso-Muth-Mills in GlaeserTerm listGlaeser

Alonso-Muth-Mills: holding income/amenities constant, distance raises transport costs → housing costs fall.

  • Boston-area towns: prices/densities fall outward, but school quality may matter more for prices.
  • Walk-or-drive choice (fixed car cost) + decentralized jobs → flatter price slope.
  • Glaeser, Kahn and Rappaport: transit access explains central poor; alternative: rich demand more land.

A.5.3Rosen-Roback in GlaeserTerm listGlaeser

Rosen-Roback: high metro housing prices reflect high incomes, amenities or both.

  • Warmer places → accepted lower real income; high-income places → offsetting negatives, including longer commutes.
  • Human capital also affects income → imprecise predictions.

A.5.4Employers: high wages need high productivityGlaeser

Glaeser: high wages require productivity benefits.

  • Ports/resources, nearby suppliers/customers; cheaper goods transport → face-to-face law/finance services.
  • Closeness speeds ideas: Marshall's "mysteries of the trade in the air"; Jacobs: new ideas combine old ones.
  • Educated populations → human capital spillovers, higher wages, faster growth.

A.5.5Builders: why supply differsGlaeser

Glaeser's builder's equilibrium: why abundant construction/low prices in some places, little construction/high prices elsewhere?

A.5.6Policy: people over placesGlaeser

Glaeser: prioritize people's choices over rebuilding places; anticipate mobile people/firms and imperfect government representation.

A.6.2Chicago: lock-in and inertia (Cronon)Slides 3a

Cronon: Chicago's factors aligned first → lock-in/inertia → primate city; St. Louis or another Midwest city could have led.

A.6.3Machine CityTerm listSlides 3a

What Machine City treats a city as a system that planners can design.

How Roads, pipes and parks can be planned.

Effect Chicago also grew through geography, inventions and historical events that cannot simply be copied.

Example / link Copying Chicago’s layout would not guarantee another Chicago.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Picture

Imagine Chicago as a machine with replaceable tracks and pipes—but no button that manufactures a Great Fire or a historical head start. Planned parts ≠ a reproducible history.

Additional course detail

Machine City: can planners recreate Chicago? Yes: networks, engineering, design. No: innovations, historic accidents, being first.

A.6.4Los Angeles: growth machineSlides 3a

Slides 3a: LA's growth machine depended on developers needing continual growth.

  • Aerospace/oil/freight/entertainment agglomeration; shipping hub.
  • Engineered water, air conditioning, "drive until you qualify" highways.

A.7Urbanization and migration (Jedwab & Vollrath)

A.7.1UrbanizationJedwab

Jedwab: urbanization = population share living in cities.

A.7.2Urbanization without GrowthTerm listJedwab

What Urbanization without growth means more people live in cities without matching income growth.

How Rural hardship, urban policies and improved survival can increase the urban share.

Effect Cities may expand while poverty remains.

Example / link Moving to a city to escape a drought does not guarantee a productive urban job.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Picture

A city’s population counter climbs while its income-per-person counter stays still. Urbanization measures the urban share; it is not an income meter.

Additional course detail

At equal GDP per capita, countries were 25–30 points more urban in 2010 than 1500. Four reasons:

  1. Safer/easier city life.
  2. Government urban bias.
  3. Worsening countryside: rural push.
  4. More urban survival: births exceed deaths.
Memory tip: S-U-R-B: Safer, Urban bias, Rural push, Births exceed deaths; sounds like "suburb".

A.7.3Rural push and urban pullTerm listJedwab

What Rural push drives people away from the countryside; urban pull attracts them to cities.

How Drought or lost livelihoods can push; jobs and education can pull.

Effect Migration can reflect hardship rather than growing urban prosperity.

Example / link Escaping rural unemployment does not mean a good city job is waiting.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Body cue

Push from behind; pull from ahead. Rural hardship pushes someone out, while city jobs pull them in. A move can be an escape, not a step into prosperity.

Additional course detail

Rural push: hardship, drought, war, no farmland drive migration; urban pull: city jobs/wages attract migrants. Urban migration does not prove rising wealth.

A.8Growth, decline and the changing economy

A.8.1Malthusian vs Solowian growthTerm list

What In a Malthusian economy, population growth can absorb income gains.

How In Solow, more capital raises output per worker but faces diminishing returns.

Effect Sustained long-run growth per person requires technological progress in the standard model.

Example / link More total output—or more urban residents—does not automatically mean richer individuals.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Sound and logic

Malthus → MOUTHS: more mouths absorb gains. Solow → skills and technology raise output per person. Do not memorize “capital alone forever”: diminishing returns matter.

Additional course detail
  • Malthusian: population growth keeps living standards flat; Jedwab: crowded cities → higher deaths, balanced wages.
  • Solowian: capital deepening raises output but faces diminishing returns; sustained long-run income-per-person growth requires technological progress (growth study guide).
Memory tip: Malthus = mouths; Solow = income soars.

A.8.2Consumption City (Jedwab; Glaeser, Kolko and Saiz)Term listJedwab

What A consumption city grows by spending income from resources such as oil.

How That money supports shops, restaurants and other local services.

Effect The city can grow without a strong manufacturing economy.

Example / link More urban jobs do not necessarily mean higher productivity: urbanization without growth.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Logic

Picture oil money paying for haircuts and restaurants, not building factories. Resource income → local spending → service jobs. Urban growth need not mean industrial growth.

Additional course detail

Consumption City: two meanings; Week 3 suggests Jedwab's.

  • Jedwab: oil/gold/diamond windfalls → urban spending → non-tradable services rather than tradable goods → missed agricultural/industrial revolutions' long-run growth.
  • Glaeser, Kolko and Saiz: restaurants, entertainment, geography attract consumption; rising city demand, faster high-amenity growth, rents rising faster than wages (paper).
Memory tip: Jedwab = windfall; Glaeser = amenities.

A.8.3Urban Economic TransitionTerm listJedwab

What Urban economic transition means a change in a city’s main industries and jobs.

How Employment may shift from farming to factories, then services.

Effect Some cities gain new work; others lose the industry they depended on.

Example / link A factory closure can hurt the whole surrounding neighborhood.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Sequence

Visualize three workplaces: field → factory → finance office. Then picture a factory town losing its main employer: transition can produce growth elsewhere and decline here.

Additional course detail

Jedwab: developed countries deindustrialize toward tradable finance/business services; manufacturing rises then falls. Single-industry cities suffer most: Detroit autos.

Memory tip: factory to finance.

A.8.4Shrinking CitiesTerm list

What A shrinking city experiences lasting population loss, often with job losses.

How Industry decline, outward migration or demographic change reduce demand.

Effect Vacant buildings and a smaller tax base make services harder to maintain.

Example / link Detroit connects industrial change to urban decline and unequal job access.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Picture

Picture a half-empty city with the same long pipes and roads. Fewer taxpayers still support a large network. That is why shrinkage creates service and maintenance problems.

Additional course detail

Shrinking Cities: falling population, economic activity and social vitality from deindustrialization, suburbanization/redlining or aging.

  • Detroit; link Grengs (Shrinking city, Detroit).

BWhat shape does the city take?

B.1Sprawl dimensions (Galster)

Galster: causes → physical pattern (eight dimensions) → consequences.

B.1.1DensityTerm listGalster

What Density counts people, homes or buildings per unit of land.

How The result depends on what and where you count.

Effect Higher density can support services, but does not guarantee walkability or good social life.

Example / link The same number of buildings can form connected streets or isolated compounds.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Formula

Density = amount ÷ area. Put the same number of buildings on two maps, one connected and one scattered: equal density does not mean equal urban form.

Additional course detail

Density = development/land area; alone cannot describe sprawl.

B.1.2ContinuityGalster

Unbroken development vs "leapfrog" gaps.

B.1.3ConcentrationGalster

Most development occupying little city area.

B.1.4ClusteringGalster

Tight bunching within an area.

B.1.5CentralityGalster

Closeness to CBD/downtown.

B.1.6NuclearityGalster

One dominant center (mononuclear) vs several.

B.1.7Mixed usesGalster

Different activities within one small area/block.

B.1.8ProximityGalster

Distances between different uses citywide.

Memory tip: Amount → gaps → spread → centers → uses.
  • Amount: Density; gaps: Continuity; spread: Concentration + Clustering.
  • Centers: Centrality + Nuclearity; uses: Mixed uses + Proximity.

B.1.9Why one word is not enoughGalster

Inconsistent dimension scores → "sprawl" hides differences.

B.2Street networks and travel

B.2.1Griddedness (Boeing)Term listBoeing

What Griddedness measures how much streets resemble a grid.

How Boeing combines orientation order, straightness and four-way intersections.

Effect Connected routes offer more choices than loops and dead ends.

Example / link Grid-like neighborhoods have lower car ownership on average; this association alone does not prove causation.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Picture

Draw a checkerboard: orderly directions, straight lines, four-way crossings. All three are needed; a few straight roads alone do not make a grid.

Additional course detail

Griddedness = orientation order + straightness + four-way intersections.

  • Connected grids → finance/engineering rules encouraged disconnected, car-oriented suburbs (loops, cul-de-sacs) → partial grid return in recent decades.
  • More grid-like neighborhoods ↔ lower car ownership: association, not proof.
  • Networks last generations → build connections.
Memory tip: Checkerboard (+), not T-junctions/dead ends.

B.2.2The "D variables" (Ewing & Cervero)Term list

What The five Ds are density, diversity, design, destination accessibility and distance to transit.

How They describe different ways the built environment shapes travel choices.

Effect Density alone cannot explain whether people can easily walk or use transit.

Example / link A dense neighborhood may still have disconnected streets and distant stations.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Five questions

How much? Density. What mix? Diversity. Which streets? Design. What can I reach? Destinations. How far to transit? Distance. Five Ds describe different features.

Additional course detail

Boeing cites Ewing & Cervero: 3 Ds = density, diversity (land-use mix), design (street network).

  • Design: intersection density, four-way intersection share.
  • Later + destination accessibility + distance to transit = 5 Ds.
  • Driving relates most to destination accessibility, then design; density weak after controlling others.
Memory tip: 3 Ds = place; 2 added Ds = getting there.

B.3Outward movement and city experience

B.3.1SuburbanizationTerm listIhlanfeldt

What Suburbanization is growth or movement toward a metropolitan area’s outskirts.

How Cheaper land, better transport, housing preferences and policy can encourage it.

Effect Homes and jobs become more spread out, often increasing car dependence.

Example / link Suburbanization is a process; sprawl describes a physical pattern.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Picture

Draw arrows from the center toward the edge: first homes, then jobs. Suburbanization is movement; sprawl is the resulting physical pattern, which needs separate description.

Additional course detail

Suburbanization = people/jobs → periphery (job decentralization).

B.3.2Pedestrian vs auto-orientation: LA vs ChicagoTerm listSlides 3a

What Pedestrian-oriented places support walking and staying; auto-oriented places prioritize driving.

How Short crossings and nearby shops help walkers; wide roads and parking serve cars.

Effect Design changes access, physical activity and street experience.

Example / link Compare walking five minutes for groceries with needing to drive.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Two viewpoints

Imagine the same street through a windshield and on foot. Big parking signs suit drivers; short crossings and active frontages suit walkers. Design chooses whose experience comes first.

Additional course detail

LA vs Chicago: pedestrian/auto-orientation, experienced density, affordability, legibility differ.

CWho gets what? (urban problems)

C.1Jobs and unequal access

C.1.1Jobs-Housing Mismatch / spatial mismatch (Ihlanfeldt, Atlanta)Term listIhlanfeldt

What Jobs-housing mismatch separates workers from suitable jobs.

How Jobs move outward while housing discrimination, transport and other barriers hold workers back.

Effect Finding work becomes harder, and commuting costs rise.

Example / link A factory moves to the suburbs while its workers remain in the inner city.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Picture

Draw a worker’s home and a job separated by a housing barrier. The job moves outward, but the worker cannot follow. Remember SPACE before thinking about transport mode.

Additional course detail

Suburban jobs + housing discrimination → Black workers cannot follow.

  • Job-information, transport and hiring barriers → unemployment, lower wages, costlier commutes/lower net earnings.
  • Count suitable jobs and competing workers.
  • Policy: move people to jobs, jobs to people, or improve information/transport connections.

C.1.2Modal Mismatch (Grengs, Detroit)Term listGrengs

What Modal mismatch means unequal job access through different transport modes.

How The same address may offer good access by car but poor access by bus.

Effect Residents without cars can be disadvantaged even near many jobs.

Example / link Grengs emphasizes MODE; spatial mismatch emphasizes WHERE homes and jobs are.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Contrast

Same home, two keys: car key versus bus pass. The address stays fixed, but reachable jobs change. Spatial mismatch asks WHERE; modal mismatch asks HOW.

Additional course detail

Same neighborhood: good job access by car, poor by transit.

  • Mobility ≠ accessibility; neighborhood averages hide mode differences (ecological fallacy).
  • Gravity model weights nearer jobs and competing workers; links cumulative/competitive access.
  • Disadvantaged residents' trip-chaining (home, childcare, work, shops) defeats simple home-work bus routes.
Memory tip: Ihlanfeldt = space; Grengs = mode.

C.2Accessibility measures

C.2.1Primal vs dual accessibilityTerm list

What Primal accessibility counts opportunities within a fixed travel time.

How Dual accessibility asks how long reaching a fixed number takes.

Effect More opportunities improve primal access; less time improves dual access.

Example / link Compare “jobs within 30 minutes” with “minutes needed to reach 100 jobs.”

Memory trick
Memory trick · Paired questions

Primal = PILE of opportunities within a fixed time. Dual = DURATION to reach a fixed pile. More is better for primal; less time is better for dual.

Additional course detail

Primal: opportunities within a time limit. Dual: time to reach a set number.

  • Examples: jobs within 20 minutes vs time to reach 2 jobs.
  • Search-summary only; verify class wording (source).
Memory tip: primal = piles; dual = duration.

C.2.2Cumulative vs competitive accessibility (Shen)Term list

What Cumulative accessibility counts reachable opportunities.

How Competitive accessibility also accounts for the people seeking them.

Effect Counting jobs alone can overstate a worker’s chances.

Example / link Ten openings mean something different with ten applicants than with a thousand.

Memory trick
Memory trick · Contrast

Cumulative: count the job vacancies. Competitive: now count the applicants too. Ten openings mean something different with ten applicants than with a thousand.

Additional course detail

Cumulative: opportunities within a travel-time threshold. Competitive (Shen): reachable jobs ÷ competitors.

  • Cumulative ignores increasing travel difficulty with time.
  • Grengs's gravity model adjusts for competition (Shen 1998 discussion).
Memory tip: count vs count ÷ competitors.

C.6.4Contact Hypothesis (Valentine)Term listValentine

Memory trick
