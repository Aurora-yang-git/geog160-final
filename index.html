A. Why a city exists, and where

What counts as a city?

There is no single definition. A city can be defined by its administrative boundary (a government's territory), its physical built-up area, its population density, its function (the area connected by commuting, trade, services), or its culture/experience (a shared sense of place). Choose the definition that fits your question. Functional and Tributary Areas means defining a city by what it does: the areas that "belong" to it, such as its commuting zone, TV market, sports fans, or medical service area.

The core idea: everyone trades land price against travel cost

Millions of small decisions (where to open a firm, where to rent) create a city's pattern. Each person maximizes "utility" (money, time, enjoyment) and must make trade-offs. Central locations have the highest land price and lowest travel cost; outer locations have the lowest land price and highest travel cost. The system settles into a balance (equilibrium). Every theory below applies that one idea.

Why Chicago? Westward expansion and land speculation, railroads, Lake Michigan/river, engineering (raising the city, reversing the river), credit networks and machine politics, accidents (the fire, the World's Fair). It became the primate city. Cronon: it could have been Minneapolis, St. Louis, Milwaukee or Detroit, but Chicago's factors lined up first, which gives lock-in and inertia.

Machine City: can planners recreate Chicago? Networks, engineering and design yes; innovations, historic accidents and being first no.

Why LA? Growth machine (developers needed continual growth), aerospace/oil/freight/entertainment agglomeration, shipping hub, engineered water, air conditioning, "drive until you qualify" highways.

Why people move to cities (Week 3, Jedwab & Vollrath)

Urbanization = share of people living in cities. At the same income, countries are 25–30 points more urban in 2010 than in 1500: Urbanization without Growth. Four reasons: the city got safer or easier to live in, government policy favors cities (urban bias), the countryside got worse (rural push), or more people survive in cities (births exceed deaths). Urban pull is the opposite: jobs and wages. Lesson: moving to a city does not tell you they became richer.

Stop and recall: (1) Chicago vs LA: centers, density, when built. (2) Why Chicago? (3) What does "urbanization without growth" mean, and what are the four reasons? (4) What does Machine City ask?

B. What shape does the city take?

Galster: sprawl has eight dimensions

Galster separates the physical pattern from its causes and effects (causes → pattern → consequences). The eight dimensions describe the pattern:

  1. Density: development per land area (alone it can't describe sprawl).
  2. Continuity: unbroken vs "leapfrog" gaps.
  3. Concentration: is most development in a small share of the area?
  4. Clustering: within an area, is development tightly bunched?
  5. Centrality: closeness to the CBD.
  6. Nuclearity: one dominant center (mononuclear) vs several.
  7. Mixed uses: different activities in the same small area.
  8. Proximity: how far apart different uses are across the city.

Cities don't score consistently across dimensions (tested on 13 urbanized areas), so one word, "sprawl", hides a lot.

Boeing: streets and griddedness

Griddedness = orientation order + straightness + four-way intersections. History: connected grids (pre-1940) → disconnected suburbs (loops, cul-de-sacs, car-oriented, encouraged by finance and engineering rules) → a partial return after 2000. More grid-like neighborhoods have lower car ownership (an association, not proof). Street networks last for generations, so planners should build in connections.

Moving outward

Suburbanization = people and jobs moving to the periphery (job decentralization).

Experience of form

LA vs Chicago differ in pedestrian vs auto-orientation, the experience of density, affordability, and legibility.

Stop and recall: List Galster's eight dimensions from memory. Which two are easy to confuse (concentration/clustering; centrality/nuclearity; mixed uses/proximity)? What three features make up griddedness?

C. Who gets what? (urban problems)

Jobs (Week 4)

  • Jobs-Housing Mismatch / spatial mismatch (Ihlanfeldt, Atlanta): jobs move to the suburbs; housing discrimination keeps Black workers from following, so their choices shrink. Barriers: don't hear about jobs, no usable transport, hiring discrimination. Harm: unemployment, lower wages, higher commuting cost (lower net earnings). Count suitable jobs and competing workers. Policy: move people to jobs, jobs to people, or improve connections (job information and transport).
  • Modal Mismatch (Grengs, Detroit): the same neighborhood gives good job access by car and very poor access by transit. Mobility ≠ accessibility. A neighborhood average hides this (ecological fallacy). The gravity model weights nearer jobs more and adjusts for competing workers (the idea behind cumulative/competitive access).

Health and the environment

  • Wilson (Week 3): zoning and planning decide who gets healthy surroundings. Exclusionary zoning keeps poorer families out of better towns; metropolitan fragmentation means no one ensures fairness across the region. Burdens (food, unsafe streets, stress, pollution; sedentary habits) pile up as a "riskscape", an environmental injustice. Redevelopment must let existing residents stay.
  • Environment (6a): cities are sinking as seas rise; extreme weather events overload drains because pavement replaces grass; heat islands come from concrete, steel and lack of shade, and air conditioning strains grids.

Housing (6a)

  • Shortage / surplus: supply = housing units; demand = population. Demand is inelastic (everyone needs a home), but can adjust through migration (Rosen-Roback), subdividing, households combining or splitting, submarkets, location (jobs-housing balance). Measure: vacancy, new construction, household formation, affordability (rent-to-income).
  • Life course: young move to cities; around 30 people form families and need a home; older people leave.
  • Filtering: houses get older and filter down while people filter up; a high-quality home for a 40-year-old becomes a starter home for a 30-year-old. Old buildings = affordable stock.
  • Segregation: housing is the main mechanism (lending discrimination, HOAs). Measured by entropy and dissimilarity indices.
  • Gentrification: wealthier inflow → displacement, gentrification-in-place, cultural erasure. It's a "wicked problem": improving a neighborhood can push out the poor. Resistance can become NIMBYism ("not in my backyard") and NIABY, leaving no development anywhere. Fixes: mixed-rent housing, participatory planning.
  • Contact Hypothesis: contact improves relations, but not when groups compete (Great Depression subdivided homes: new nonwhite residents blamed for falling quality). Valentine (Week 5): proximity ≠ interaction; politeness ≠ changed beliefs; tolerance ≠ respect; good experiences become "exceptions"; micro-publics (shared activities) may help; meaningful contact must scale up.
Stop and recall: Spatial mismatch vs modal mismatch in one sentence each. What is the housing "filtering" idea? Three outcomes of gentrification? What does Valentine say about contact?

D. How did planners respond? (planning movements)

Pattern: planning follows crises. The Industrial Revolution (1800s) brought fast urbanization with no governance (laissez-faire): overcrowding, disease, inequality. Two responses: utopian movements ("solve everything at once") and technical officials ("one problem at a time").

  1. Sanitary Movement: answer to overcrowding and disease; public health; origin of zoning for health.
  2. City Beautiful (early 1900s US; Burnham, Robinson, Olmsted): beauty, cleanliness, leisure space; park-and-boulevard systems; first large-scale modern planning; municipal bureaucracy. Criticized: surface aesthetics, top-down, ignored housing and population, short-lived. Legacy: parks, Beaux-Arts buildings, municipal power.
  3. Garden City (late 1800s England; Howard, Unwin, Parker): decentralized, small self-sufficient towns with green belts and communal land; more social reform than City Beautiful. But Letchworth shifted to residential design, so principles went into suburbs. Neighborhood Unit (Clarence Perry) appears in this section.
  4. Modernism (1920s on): top-down, rational, ornament-free, geometric order, single-use zones, same everywhere. Le Corbusier's Radiant City (1935): "get people off the streets into parks". After 1950 it meant highways, clearance, urban renewal, public housing, car dependence. The alternative: Frank Lloyd Wright's Broadacre City (1932–35): decentralized, an acre per family, car-based, nature woven in, infinitely repeatable.
  5. Jane Jacobs (1961, Death and Life of Great American Cities): against Modernism and Robert Moses (Lower Manhattan Expressway). Street life is complicated order, not chaos: sidewalk ballet, eyes on the street. Week 5 reading: safety from informal public supervision, with three conditions (clear public/private boundaries, eyes on the street, fairly continuous sidewalk use); shops add walkers, watchers and attention.
  6. Urban Vitality: street activity or a built environment that supports it. Mechanism: built environment → mixed population → active all day → sidewalk contacts → safety and cohesion. Six dimensions: density, small blocks, mixed primary uses, aged buildings, accessibility, no border vacuums.
  7. Gehl, Lynch, Whyte, Oldenburg (human-scale thinkers, see E).
  8. New Urbanism (Duany & Plater-Zyberk, 1991): walkability, connectivity, mixed use, density, green space, traditional neighborhood design (Seaside, Florida).
  9. Transit-Oriented Development (Calthorpe, 1993): dense mixed use and short blocks within ~10 minutes of stations.
  10. Urban Acupuncture (Lerner, Curitiba) / Tactical Urbanism: small, cheap, temporary interventions (guerrilla, DIY, pop-up) instead of master plans; follows Lynch.

Critiques of Jacobs: NIMBYism and gentrification; hard to measure; she described what happened naturally (can't just copy it: Alexander, "A City is Not a Tree"); mixed empirical results (more walking, but maybe only necessary walking; mixed use raises social cohesion, density lowers it).

Stop and recall (draw it): Write the planning chain from Sanitary to Tactical Urbanism on paper, then add who opposed whom. For Modernism and Jacobs, say one city-scale and one human-scale effect.

E. How do people feel the city?

Public space thinkers (7a)

  • Jan Gehl (1971): necessary / optional / resultant activities. Poor spaces get only necessary ones; good spaces get lingering and social ones. Low-intensity contacts. Auto-oriented streets kill optional activity. Not sure about grids; skeptical of extreme density.
  • Kevin Lynch (1981): city as a learning ecology; provide a good space and let people improve it. Dimensions: vitality, sense (legibility), fit, access, control, efficiency, justice. Bottom-up, not comprehensive.
  • William Whyte (1980): ten years watching NYC plazas. People attract people; effective capacity; people use spaces their own way; seating and food matter; groups choose good places. Triangulation: strangers talk when both look at the same thing. Also: keeping out "undesirables" keeps out everyone (hostile architecture).
  • Ray Oldenburg (1989): third places: neutral, leveling, conversation, accessible, regulars, low profile, playful, at-home. Cares about the place's character and regulars more than foot traffic.

Urban sociology

  • Simmel (1903): stimulation overload → blasé attitude as a "protective organ".
  • Wirth (1938): size, density, heterogeneity → a collective personality.
  • Goffman: the interaction order; civil inattention (nod, brief apology, looking away). Lofland: it is politeness, not coldness. Morrill: "together alone".
  • Putnam, "Bowling Alone" (2000): social capital fell. Aelbrecht: new spaces (malls, gas stations) emerged.
  • Loneliness vs solitude: contacts and loneliness are weakly related; solitude is comfortable being alone.

Borer (Week 6): experiencing the city

Urbanism (urban lives and culture) vs urbanization (growth). Sensing is social; locational socialization: shared activity gives a place its meaning. Three ways in: Simmel (blasé), Benjamin (the flâneur, a stroller reading the street), Goffman (face-saving, interaction order). Five sensescapes: sight (landmarks, readability; Gans's West End misread as a slum and demolished), sound (keynote, soundmarks, sound signals; noise depends on listener), smell (cultural judgments, e.g. "stench of the poor"), taste (Bourdieu: taste marks status; Gans: taste cultures; omnivores), touch (walking, parkour, and unequal freedom of movement).

Stop and recall: Gehl's three activities; Lynch's key contribution; Whyte's triangulation; Oldenburg vs Jacobs; Simmel vs Goffman; Borer's five senses.

Reading one-liners (for essays)

  • Dexter: land use and transport shape each other. Glaeser: people, firms and builders respond to costs until places balance.
  • Galster: sprawl = eight dimensions of physical pattern. Boeing: streets left the grid and partly returned.
  • Jedwab & Vollrath: urbanization alone doesn't show growth. Wilson: planning and zoning decide who gets healthy surroundings.
  • Ihlanfeldt: segregation + suburban jobs. Grengs: car vs transit access in Detroit.
  • Jacobs: safe streets come from eyes and continuous use. Valentine: contact ≠ respect. Borer: the city is sensed and socially made.

City scale vs human scale (say this in every answer)

City scale: planners solved big problems (disease, congestion, housing) with comprehensive plans. Human scale: those plans often ignored daily experience, so Jacobs, Gehl, Whyte, Lynch pushed back. The best answers show both.
